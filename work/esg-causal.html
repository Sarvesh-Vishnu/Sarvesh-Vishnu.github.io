§ Project 06

Correlation, minus the confound.

Independent research · Primary researcher · 2024 · S&P 500 panel

R · Excel · Propensity score matching · Difference-in-difference · Firm and year fixed effects

The problem

Most of the literature on ESG and financial performance is a graveyard of correlational claims. Firms that score high on ESG also tend to be larger, more profitable, and concentrated in specific sectors. The interesting question isn't do high-ESG firms perform better? — it's does becoming better on ESG cause better financial performance? You can't answer that with a scatter plot.

Approach

Standard causal inference toolkit, applied carefully. Propensity score matching to construct plausible counterfactuals, difference-in-difference for firms that shifted ESG posture, and firm + year fixed effects to strip out time-invariant confounders. Every specification choice documented, and every robustness check reported — not just the ones that supported the headline.

  • Matched treated firms to controls on size, sector, and pre-treatment financials
  • Estimated DiD with firm and year fixed effects
  • Ran the analysis under multiple specifications and reported the range, not a point estimate
  • Documented every choice so the analysis is reproducible end-to-end

Outcome

A causal estimate defensible under scrutiny. It's a smaller number than the correlational studies typically report — which is exactly what you'd expect once you're accounting for selection. The output isn't a headline; it's a range with its assumptions attached, which is what a decision-maker actually needs.

What I'd do next

Decompose the effect by ESG pillar — environmental, social, governance. The average masks a lot; the mechanism matters more than the topline. And extend to a longer post-treatment window to see whether the effect compounds, decays, or reverses.
