Real data: EPA Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program (facility emissions and parent companies, public domain) and SEC company filings. The code and the firm-name crosswalk are in the project folder; every estimate on this page is read from its output.

Objective

Companies are told that sustainability pays: lower emissions, lower risk, better returns. At Duke our team tested that claim on S&P 500 firms with ESG scores, using regression, matching and fixed effects. Two years on I rebuilt the study from scratch, to answer the same question with data anyone can check and with the mistakes we made fixed.

What the first study taught me

  • The matching result was reported as a significant negative effect, but its own 95% interval ran from −65.6 to +26.4. It could not tell an effect from none.
  • The outcome was the stock price, which depends on how many shares a firm has issued and says little about performance.
  • Licensed ESG scores arrive as a single number with an unclear direction: some providers score risk, where higher is worse.

Approach

  • Measured the “E” directly: each firm’s reported greenhouse-gas emissions from the EPA’s facility data, 2011 to 2023, assigned to parent companies by their ownership shares.
  • Matched EPA parent companies to S&P 500 firms by normalised name, with a short list of renames checked by hand (Dominion Resources, FPL Group, Vistra Energy). Subsidiaries owned by others before an acquisition were left out.
  • Took revenue, net income, assets and liabilities from each firm’s 10-K filings through the SEC’s XBRL API, aligned to calendar years.
  • Treatment: emissions intensity, tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per $1M of revenue. Outcome: return on assets the following year, so profit cannot cause this year’s emissions.
  • Three estimators: OLS within sector and year; matching of low-intensity firm-years to look-alike high-intensity ones in the same sector and year; and firm and year fixed effects, which compare each firm only with itself.
  • Standard errors clustered by firm throughout, and a firm-level bootstrap for matching.
  • Three checks on the strongest estimate: emissions with revenue held fixed, the sample without utilities, and a placebo that asks whether intensity “predicts” the year before.

What I found

All three estimators point the same way: firms that halve their emissions intensity earn slightly more the next year, about a tenth to a quarter of a point of return on assets, against an average of 5.5%. None of the three can rule out no effect.

The checks explain the fixed-effects estimate, the closest to significant. Intensity divides emissions by revenue, so a firm whose sales grow looks cleaner and more profitable in the same year. Holding revenue fixed, the effect of emissions falls to −0.02 points. And intensity “predicts” last year’s profit about as well as next year’s, which a cause running from emissions to profit could not do.

The honest answer from these data is that we cannot see profit following lower emissions within a year. That is not evidence that sustainability has no value; it is evidence that a one-year accounting return is the wrong place to look for it.

Limits

The EPA covers direct emissions from US facilities only, while revenue is global, so multinational firms look cleaner than they are. Firms that report no large facilities are not in the study. The firm list is today’s S&P 500, so firms that left the index are missing. The EPA’s latest public data is for 2023.

What I’d do next

Use stock returns and the cost of debt as outcomes, where markets might price emissions faster than accounting does. Then use timing: firms that adopted a public emissions-reduction target in different years allow a staggered difference-in-differences design, comparing each adopter with firms that had not yet adopted.
