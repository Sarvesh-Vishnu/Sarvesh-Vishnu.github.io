Objective

Annual planning across several business units of a global medical-device company is a needle-threading exercise between top-line growth targets, sales-force capacity, and product-mix reality. The team required a data spine that could support all of these facets while enabling predictive and prescriptive analytics.

Building the spine

I started as a Data Scientist on the Commercial Excellence team. I built the Python pipelines and Microsoft Fabric repositories that became the data spine, organised the team's analytics resources in one place, and built Power BI reporting for each sales team, while supporting marketing analytics.

The annual operating plan

I own the analytics behind the Annual Operating Plan end to end for three business units: medical devices in the United States, and contraception and stem-cell storage worldwide. That covers quota allocation for each unit's sales teams, ensemble forecasting across 25+ product lines, and the pipelines and executive surfaces that hold all of it together.

The forecasts it replaced were largely linear. Shipments also understate demand: an order that is cancelled, returned or left on backorder never shows up as a sale. So I rebuilt an unconstrained demand series from sales orders together with open, cancelled, returned and backordered lines, and let product characteristics into the models.

  • Rebuilt the AOP process end to end, from transaction records to executive readouts
  • Created a Power BI, SQL and Azure surface that replaced ad-hoc pulls with multiple live views
  • Standardized ensemble forecasting (ARIMA, SARIMAX, Prophet, boosting algorithms) across 25+ product lines
  • Ran scenario and sensitivity analysis to stress-test decisions before they hit a slide
  • Gave demand planning and manufacturing a planning interval for each product line, not a single number

A cross-sell experiment, end to end

The Total Orders dashboard was the first business view of products being sold across business units, which made cross-selling opportunities visible for the first time. I led the initiative that followed from start to finish: identifying the target customers, setting up the experiment as an A/B test, building the compensation plan, and evaluating the outcome.

Accounts were randomised within ten territories, about 200 accounts in each, and the test ran for about four months. The five metrics were ranked before it started: cross-sell orders first, then new-product orders, revenue per account, time to first order and attach rate, so no metric could be promoted after the results came in.

The test found no meaningful lift. Read alongside a bundling analysis across the organisation and a change in leadership, that result helped move the strategy: the sales group now leads with a single product, and the other products moved to other sales teams.

Alongside it: customer segmentation for a product line, regulatory analytics support, and data extractions to support organisational compliance.

Strategy and Market Insights

I then moved to the Strategy and Market Insights team as a Senior Data Scientist. The questions are a level up: how the largest businesses are doing, what external projects are worth, SKU-by-country forecasts for supply chain and demand planning, long-range business planning, price elasticity, and market research. Most recently that includes price elasticity for the fertility business.

The research companion

Alongside the planning work I built a research companion on LangGraph that writes leadership a sourced brief every Monday from public sources only. It has its own case study.

Outcome

Forecast error fell from about 18% to 8% WAPE against the linear forecasts it replaced, mostly from modelling the demand customers asked for rather than the demand that shipped. That is error month by month; over the full year, misses in either direction largely cancel, and each product line's actuals landed within 5% of its forecast, and demand planning and manufacturing now plan from intervals instead of single numbers.

The cross-sell test answered its question before a plan was scaled up: no meaningful lift. The sales structure changed with that evidence in hand.

Reporting moved from ad-hoc pulls to live Power BI views, and product-launch decisions are tested against the forecast before they become commitments.

What I'd do next

A closed loop between the forecast and the sales system so drift is caught within a cycle instead of at year-end reconciliation. And more automated attribution, separating pricing, mix, and volume effects at the product-line level, so when a number moves, we know why.
